# Reading the source

A post is only as good as the reading behind it. Read the whole source before writing a word, and write only from what you read and what the user said. Never write about a source from its title, a search snippet or what you remember about it.

## By kind of source

**A link to an article, report or page.** Open it with the web tool and read all of it. Note the title, the publisher, the author, the date and the one or two claims that matter. If the page won't open (a paywall, a login wall, a page that blocks tools), say so and ask the user to paste the text. Don't fall back on other copies of it around the web.

**A LinkedIn post or article.** These usually need a login, so ask the user to paste the text and say who wrote it. If the user wants to answer it, follow "Answering someone else's post" below.

**An idea, opinion or question.** The user's words are the source. If they haven't said what made them think it, Step 3 of SKILL.md asks. A post built on an idea alone, with no case, number or decision behind it, is the kind LinkedIn cuts. If the idea is a question the user wants answered, answer it from what they give you and say where the answer needs a fact they'd have to supply.

**An uploaded document (PDF, Word, slides, spreadsheet).** Read it with the file tools. For a long report, read the summary and the sections the user points to, and list the page or slide each fact comes from. For a spreadsheet, compute any figure from the rows; never estimate one by eye.

**An image or screenshot.** Describe only what it shows. Read any text in it exactly. A chart's numbers come from its labels, not from guessing bar heights.

**A transcript, talk or podcast.** Machine transcripts garble names and numbers. Flag every quote, name and number from one for the user to check before posting.

**Notes, an email thread or a draft.** Treat them as the user's own material. Their old post or draft is material too: keep a line from it only when its fact is still true.

**The user's past posts.** These are voice samples, not facts for the new post. Match their length, their paragraph habits and their words.

## What to write down

Keep a scratch list, out of the post:

- What the source says, and who says it.
- The date of the source. If it's more than a year old and the post talks about "now," flag it.
- What the user gave: their facts, their numbers, their view.
- What's missing that the post would need.

## Credit and quoting

- Name the source in the post: the publisher, author or speaker. "A Fortune piece this week" is enough when it's true.
- Quote at most one short line, under 15 words, in quotation marks, word for word. Put everything else in your own words.
- Never let the post stand in for the source. A summary of someone else's article, with no view of the user's own, gives readers no reason to read the post and gives LinkedIn a reason to bury it.

## Answering someone else's post

- State what they said fairly, in their words where you can.
- Credit what they got right before disputing or extending it.
- Argue with the idea on the record, never with a private exchange.
- Tag the person only when you'd be glad for them to read how the post treats them.
