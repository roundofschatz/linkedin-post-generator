# Picking the post type

Start from what the material is, then tell the user in one line why you picked the type. In 2026 data, documents and multi-image posts drew more engagement than text, and link posts drew the least (`references/platform.md`). Text stays the skill's default because a document needs material that fills slides; when the material has that substance, pick the document.

| The material is | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The writer's own article, paper, page or launch, to send readers to | Sharing your own work | The link is the point. |
| A view, a story or a lesson | Text | The words are the whole post. |
| A framework, a process, steps or a checklist the reader will keep | Document (PDF carousel) | One idea per slide, and the top format for engagement in 2026 data. |
| Real photos of the work, an event or a before-and-after | Multiple images | Only when the user has the images. |
| A real choice the writer wants an answer to | Poll | Only when the writer will use the answer. |
| Something the writer will film | Video | Only when the video and the text say the same thing. |
| More than 3,000 characters of argument | Article or newsletter | Too long for a post. |

## Sharing your own work

The post's job is to get the right people to open the piece. It previews the argument and sends readers to the evidence.

- **First line:** the piece's central question or claim, across everything the piece covers. If the piece compares three industries, the line names all three. Never open on one example from inside the piece.
- **Body, two or three short paragraphs:** what the piece argues, in the writer's own words, and what a reader will get from it, like a set of questions, a model or a finding they can use. Leave the evidence, the cases and the statistics in the piece. If one example earns a place, give it two full sentences or leave it out.
- **The link** on its own line after the body, written out in full. Tell the user to remove the preview card LinkedIn adds when they paste it: in one study, posts with a preview card got about half the reach of link-free posts, against a smaller cost for a plain link.
- **The other route:** when the piece has a framework or findings that would fill slides, offer a document post that teaches them, in one line. Documents drew the most engagement in 2026 data; link posts drew the least.
- **Close:** a specific question about the piece's subject that someone in the field can answer in a sentence.
- **Length:** 1,300 to 1,900 characters, the same as a text post, since posts in that range did best in 2026 data. Spend the length on the argument, not on evidence.
- When the user gives their own draft, keep their framing and terms. Fix the opening and cut what's vague; don't swap their words for the piece's or yours.

## Text

Follow the build in SKILL.md: 1,300 to 1,900 characters, the point in the first line, short paragraphs, one subject.

## Document (PDF carousel)

Deliver two things: the post text and a slide outline.

- **Post text:** 150 to 1,300 characters, the skill's choice since no data covers it. Say what the slides teach and why it matters, then stop. The slides hold the detail.
- **Slides:** one idea per slide, a short line or two each, in words a reader takes in at a glance.
  - Slide 1 states the promise of the deck in plain words: what the reader will be able to do.
  - The middle slides take one step, part or example each, from the user's material.
  - The last slide states the takeaway, or the one question the writer wants answered.
  - The research suggests 6 to 10 slides for a framework deck. Let the material set the number.
- Ask whether the user wants the slides built as a file. If yes, build a PDF, one slide per page, at 1080 by 1350 pixels. That size is the skill's choice, a common 4:5 carousel page; the research doesn't cover page size. LinkedIn takes the PDF through "Add a document," and asks for a title, which the user should set to slide 1's line.

## Multiple images

- Post text: 150 to 1,300 characters.
- Use only the user's own images. Say what each one shows, in order, and write a short alt text for each: what's in it, in one plain sentence.
- Never suggest stock photos, and never describe an image the user didn't give.

## Poll

- Post text: 150 to 1,300 characters. Say why the writer is asking and what they'll do with the answer.
- LinkedIn's limits: the question runs up to 140 characters, with two to four options of up to 30 characters each (`references/platform.md`).
- The options cover the real choices and don't overlap. Skip "It depends" unless the writer will follow up on it.
- Write the poll into its own file for the check: the question on line 1, one option per line after it.

## Video

- Write a short script only when the user will film. Open on the point, and keep the script to what the post text says.
- Post text: 150 to 1,300 characters.
- LinkedIn cuts the reach of video that has nothing to do with the text, so never pair a post with an unrelated clip.

## Article or newsletter

A piece that needs more than 3,000 characters isn't a post. Say so, and offer either a post that makes the single strongest point, or the long piece. For the long piece, use Plainspeak Writer's think-piece guide when it's installed.
