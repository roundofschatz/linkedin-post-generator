# What LinkedIn's feed rewards and punishes

Last checked: October 21, 2026. Only 2026 sources count here, except where a line says otherwise. When today is more than six months past that date, tell the user once that LinkedIn may have changed since, and offer to check.

Each rule names its evidence:

- **[LinkedIn]**: LinkedIn said it, on its own blog or through an executive, as reported by the news coverage listed under Sources.
- **[Data]**: a large independent study shows it. Most come from tool vendors whose samples lean toward their own users.
- **[Opinion]**: an expert's view or a small sample.

## How the feed decides who sees a post

- **One AI model now finds posts by meaning.** In March 2026, LinkedIn replaced its mix of feed systems with one model. It reads what a post is about and matches it to members whose work, skills and reading fit, inside or outside the writer's network. **[LinkedIn]** So: say the subject plainly, in the words people in the field use, and stay on one subject per post.
- **A second model ranks posts from each reader's history.** It reads a member's last thousand-plus actions in order. It predicts four things: long reads, likes, comments and shares. **[LinkedIn]** So: a post earns reach by holding the right reader to the end and starting replies.
- **The writer's profile is part of the match.** The model reads profile data such as headline, skills and work history. **[LinkedIn]** Posts that fit what the profile says the writer does match best. **[Opinion]**
- **Fresh posts surface within minutes when a topic is moving.** **[LinkedIn]**
- **Raw reach is down; fit is up.** Reach for active writers fell sharply over two years, by 47% to 60% depending on the tracker. **[Data]** Judge a post by who replied, not by views.

## What LinkedIn cuts

- **Generic, AI-sounding posts.** Since May 2026, posts that look AI-made and state no clear view travel less beyond the writer's network. By October 2026, LinkedIn's detector screened every post shown outside the writer's network. LinkedIn claims it's right 94% of the time, which means about 6 in 100 flagged posts are flagged wrongly. Members can mark a post "Seems like AI slop," and LinkedIn tells the writer when they do. Flagged posts got 40% fewer views. **[LinkedIn]** LinkedIn has also cut reach for stock AI shapes such as "it's not X, it's Y." **[LinkedIn, via Engadget]** The defense is substance only the writer has: their own case, number, decision or opinion.
- **Engagement bait.** "Comment YES if you agree," like-if and repost-if lines, and requests to type a word for a download. **[LinkedIn]**
- **Video that has nothing to do with the text.** **[LinkedIn]**
- **Recycled advice with nothing new in it.** **[LinkedIn]**
- **Automated comments and engagement pods.** Comments posted by scripts or browser plugins are hidden from the default view, and repeat use can restrict an account. **[LinkedIn]**
- **Comments that only restate the post.** **[LinkedIn]**

## Building the post

- **The opening.** The post folds behind "see more" at about 140 characters on mobile and 210 on desktop. **[Data]** The first line has to state the point.
- **Hook type.** In a 2026 sample of about 310,000 posts, story openers did best and contrarian openers came next. Question openers did worst. **[Data]**
- **Length.** In a study of about 370,000 posts, text posts of 1,300 to 2,500 characters drew more engagement than posts under 400. **[Data]** This skill aims for 1,300 to 1,900 characters, which also fits Plainspeak Writer's LinkedIn range of 150 to 300 words. The limit is 3,000 characters. **[Data]**
- **Paragraphs.** Short paragraphs and plain, short words did better in one 2026 dataset of about 400,000 posts. **[Data, one source]** A stack of one-sentence lines is a known AI and ghostwriter tell. **[Opinion]**
- **Comments over likes.** LinkedIn predicts comments as their own outcome. **[LinkedIn]** Claims such as "a comment is worth 15 likes" have no published method. **[Opinion]** A specific question that someone with experience can answer in a sentence starts replies; "Thoughts?" is bait.
- **Saves.** Reference posts such as checklists, frameworks and data tables get saved. The claim that a save is worth five likes is unconfirmed. **[Opinion]**
- **Replying.** Writers who replied within 30 minutes got about 64% more comments and 2.3 times the views. **[Data, as reported]**

## Links

- A link in the post costs reach. Estimates run from about 15% to 25% for a typed link, and about half for a link-preview card. The highest estimate is about 60%, and one dataset found links helped. **[Data, sources disagree]**
- Moving the link to the first comment may cost reach too. The data disagrees. **[Data, unsettled]**
- So: by default, name the source in the post and keep the link in the notes for the writer to decide.

## Hashtags

- Members can no longer follow hashtags, and hashtags no longer show on profiles or in search suggestions. **[Data, from 2026 guides]**
- LinkedIn's only on-record view is from July 2025: hashtags help with search, not with reach, and writers shouldn't overdo them. **[LinkedIn, pre-2026]** LinkedIn gives no number.
- 2026 sources range from "use none" to "use three to five." **[Opinion]** This skill uses none by default, and at most two that name the exact topic or a real community or event. Plainspeak Writer's LinkedIn guide sets the same cap.

## Post types, by engagement

- **Company pages, 2024 to 2025 data:** documents (PDF carousels) did best, then multi-image posts, video and text. Link posts did worst. **[Data]**
- **Company pages, 2026 quarters:** multi-image led in Q2 2026, with documents close behind. Video engagement fell in Q3. **[Data]**
- **Personal profiles:** documents got 39% more reach than the average post in one 3-million-post sample. **[Data]**
- These figures mostly measure company pages, not personal profiles. Choose the type by what the material is, then by these numbers.

## Timing and frequency

- Two or three posts a week did better per post than daily posting, which cut reach per post by about a quarter. **[Data]**
- Weekday afternoons led in Buffer's 4.8-million-post study (July 2026), and late morning through afternoon led in Sprout Social's 2026 data. **[Data, studies differ]** The writer should test two time slots.
- Posting on the same two or three subjects builds the topic match the feed looks for. Long silences, and jumping between subjects, weaken it. **[Data, as reported]**

## Being tested, not launched

- "For You" and "Network" feed tabs were in test on mobile in September 2026. **[LinkedIn]** If they launch, a post needs a real network for one tab and a clear subject for the other.
- Many 2026 guides say a model called "360Brew" runs the feed. LinkedIn's March 2026 engineering post doesn't use that name. A LinkedIn engineering VP reportedly said it was tested and dropped. Don't build advice on it.
