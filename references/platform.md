# What LinkedIn's feed rewards and punishes

Last checked: October 21, 2026. Only 2026 sources count here, except where a line says otherwise. When today is more than six months past that date, tell the user once that LinkedIn may have changed since, and offer to check.

Each rule names its evidence:

- **[LinkedIn]**: LinkedIn said it, on its own blog or through an executive, as reported by the news coverage listed under Sources.
- **[Data]**: a large independent study shows it. Most come from tool vendors whose samples lean toward their own users.
- **[Reported]** or **[Inference]**: coverage or reasoning, not a measurement.
- **[Opinion]**: an expert's view or a small sample.
- **[Report advice]**: the research report's own recommendation.

The last section lists the skill's own choices, which aren't findings.

## How the feed decides who sees a post

- **One AI model now finds posts by meaning.** In March 2026, LinkedIn replaced its mix of feed systems with one model. It reads what a post is about and matches it to members whose work, skills and reading fit, inside or outside the writer's network. **[LinkedIn]** Posts that fit current conversations in a field may travel farther, and coverage warned that each post may also get less overall reach. **[Reported]** So: say the subject plainly, in the words people in the field use, and stay on one subject per post.
- **A second model ranks posts from each reader's history.** It reads a member's last thousand-plus actions in order, and it's trained to predict long reads, likes, comments and shares, among other actions. **[LinkedIn]** So: a post earns reach by holding the right reader to the end and starting replies.
- **The writer's profile is part of the match.** The model reads profile data such as headline, skills and work history. **[LinkedIn]** Posts that fit what the profile says the writer does should match best. **[Inference]**
- **Stay in a lane.** Posting on the same two or three subjects, ones the profile backs up, builds the topic match the feed looks for; one 2026 study calls it a "topic fingerprint." Off-topic posts and comments, and long silences, weaken it. **[LinkedIn + Data; strongly supported]**
- **Fresh posts surface within minutes when a topic is moving.** **[LinkedIn]**
- **Raw reach is down.** Trackers put the drop for active writers at 47% to 60% over two years; they agree on the direction, not the size. **[Data]** Judge a post by who replied, not by views.

## What LinkedIn cuts

- **Generic, AI-looking posts with no clear point of view.** Since May 2026, these are less likely to be shown widely beyond the writer's network. They're held back, not removed. **[LinkedIn]** By October 2026, LinkedIn said its detector covered every post shown outside the writer's network, at 94% precision. That implies about 6 in 100 flagged posts may be flagged wrongly, by LinkedIn's own labels; no one has checked it independently. **[LinkedIn]**
- **The "Seems like AI slop" button.** Members can mark a post with it, and LinkedIn tells the writer when they do. LinkedIn says posts it classifies as AI slop now get 40% fewer views. It didn't give the view counts, said no single piece of feedback decides distribution, and one outlet questioned whether the drop came from the button at all. **[LinkedIn, disputed cause]**
- **Stock AI shapes.** LinkedIn has cut reach for common AI phrasing such as "it's not X, it's Y." **[LinkedIn, via Engadget]**
- The defense against all three is substance only the writer has: a named situation (with permission), their own numbers, a dated decision, or a clear and arguable opinion. **[LinkedIn, as reported]**
- **Engagement bait.** LinkedIn's named examples: "Comment 'Yes' if you agree," like-if-you-agree lines, and requests to type a word to get something sent. **[LinkedIn]**
- **Video that has nothing to do with the text.** **[LinkedIn]**
- **Recycled thought leadership that repeats common wisdom.** **[LinkedIn]**
- **Automated comments and engagement pods.** As reported, comments posted by scripts or plugins are removed from the default "Most relevant" view, may be kept from traveling beyond the commenter's network, and repeat use may restrict an account. LinkedIn's Help Center says it "may limit the visibility" of such comments. **[LinkedIn, as reported]**
- **Comments that only restate the post.** **[LinkedIn]**

## Building the post

- **The fold.** The post folds behind "see more" at about 140 characters on mobile and 210 on desktop, and most reading happens on mobile. **[Data]** The point has to land in the first 140 characters.
- **Hook type.** In a sample of about 310,000 posts (December 2025 to May 2026), story openers did best (2.60% median engagement), contrarian openers next (2.31%) and question openers worst (2.16%). The gaps are small and only three types were compared. **[Data, secondary, via Ordinal]**
- **Length.** In a study of about 370,000 posts, posts of 1,300 to 2,500 characters drew 2.61% to 2.67% median engagement, against 2.10% for posts under 400. The sample is AuthoredUp's own users. The limit is 3,000 characters. **[Data]**
- **Paragraphs.** In one dataset of about 400,000 posts, posts with fewer than 7 paragraphs did 66% worse than posts with more than 14, and posts whose words averaged more than 5 letters did 40% worse. **[Data, one source]**
- **Comments.** LinkedIn's ranker predicts comments as their own outcome. **[LinkedIn]** Claims such as "a comment is worth 15 likes" have no published method. **[Opinion]** LinkedIn is cutting low-value and automated comments, so the close should invite a real answer: a specific question, or a position people will want to add to or push back on. **[LinkedIn + report advice]**
- **Saves.** Reference posts such as checklists, frameworks and data tables are worth making for saves; the claim that a save is worth five likes is unconfirmed. **[Opinion and secondary data]**
- **Replying.** Writers who replied within 30 minutes got about 64% more comments and 2.3 times the views. **[Data, as reported]** The report's advice: stay 30 to 60 minutes and reply properly to every serious comment.

## Links

- A link in the post costs reach, but nobody agrees how much. One body link cut median reach by 18.8% in one 1.3-million-post study; a typed URL sat 21% below the same writer's link-free posts in another (up from 12%). Two sources put the cost near 60%, and one 400,000-post dataset found posts with one to three links got 43% more reach. **[Data, sources disagree]**
- **A link-preview card costs more.** Posts with an attached preview card got about half the reach of link-free posts: 414 against 795 median impressions. **[Data, one source]** So when a link stays in the post, remove the preview card LinkedIn adds, and keep the link as plain text.
- Moving the link to the first comment may cost reach too; the trackers disagree. **[Data, unsettled]**
- The report's own advice is to keep links out of the body, or to accept the reach cost, or to use a document or native post instead. **[Report advice]**

## Hashtags

- Members can no longer follow hashtags, and hashtags no longer show on profiles or in search suggestions. **[Data, from 2026 guides]**
- LinkedIn's only on-record view is from July 2025: hashtags help with search, not with reach, and writers shouldn't overdo them. **[LinkedIn, pre-2026]** LinkedIn gives no number.
- One 2026 measure found 1 to 3 relevant hashtags gave about 9% more reach and 12.6% more engagement, with falling returns at 6 or more; its method isn't published. **[Data, weak]** Advice ranges from none to three to five. The report's safer reading is 0 to 3. **[Opinion]**

## Post types, by engagement

- **Company pages, 2024 to 2025 data (1.3 million posts):** documents 7.00% engagement, multi-image 6.45%, video 6.00%, text 4.5%, links 3.25%. **[Data]**
- **Company pages, 2026 quarters:** multi-image led in Q2 (6.90%), documents close behind (6.60%), text 3.95%, links 3.30%. Video engagement fell in Q3, and video views were down 36% year over year. **[Data]**
- **AuthoredUp, 3 million posts (whose posts isn't stated):** documents got 39% more reach and 30% more engagement than the average post. **[Data]**
- The report's advice: favor documents and multi-image posts when there's real substance, and use video only when it fits the message. **[Report advice]**

## Timing and frequency

- Going from one post a week to two to four added about 1,234 impressions per post, and daily posting cut reach per post by 26%. **[Data, with disagreement; via a consultant's summary]**
- Weekday afternoons (3 to 8 p.m.) led in Buffer's 4.8-million-post study (July 2026), and late morning through afternoon led in Sprout Social's 2026 data. **[Data, studies differ]** The writer should test two time slots.

## Limits LinkedIn sets

- Post: 3,000 characters. Poll question: 140 characters. Poll options: 2 to 4, up to 30 characters each. Source: AuthoredUp's character-limit guide, updated August 2026. **[Data]**

## Being tested, not launched

- "For You" and "Network" feed tabs were in test on mobile in September 2026. **[LinkedIn]** If they launch, a post needs a real network for one tab and a clear subject for the other.
- Many 2026 guides say a model called "360Brew" runs the feed. LinkedIn's March 2026 engineering post doesn't use that name. A LinkedIn engineering VP reportedly said it was tested and dropped. Don't build advice on it.

## The skill's own choices

These rules aren't findings. Each is a choice the skill makes, with its reason.

- **Text post length, 1,300 to 1,900 characters, aiming for 1,500 to 1,700.** The bottom of the research range, so it also fits Plainspeak Writer's LinkedIn range of 150 to 300 words.
- **Paragraphs of one to three full sentences, with no paragraph-count target.** One vendor dataset found posts with more than 14 paragraphs beat posts with fewer than 7. In a 1,300 to 2,500 character post, 14 paragraphs means one short sentence per line, so the finding measures that layout. It comes from one source with a private method, and the layout is the one Plainspeak Writer bars as an AI tell, so the skill doesn't follow it.
- **Text over a document, images, a poll or a video: 150 to 1,300 characters.** No research covers this. The media holds the content, so the text stays shorter than a text post.
- **A post sharing the writer's own work keeps its link.** The research says a link costs reach. When the post exists to send readers to the work, the skill counts the clicks as worth that cost, removes the preview card to keep the cost lower, and offers a document post as the other route.
- **Hashtags: none by default, at most two.** Inside the report's 0 to 3; two matches Plainspeak Writer's cap.
- **Text is the default type** unless the material is a framework, steps or a set of images, because a document needs material that fills slides.
- **Bait beyond LinkedIn's named examples** ("Thoughts?", "Agree?", "follow for more", "tag someone"): warned, because Plainspeak Writer bars them and the report's template rules out "Agree?".
- **Opening greetings and setups** ("Happy Monday," "I've been thinking"): warned, because they spend the 140 characters before the fold.
- **Arrow or emoji bullets, and bold or italic Unicode letters:** warned, because Plainspeak Writer bars the bullets and screen readers can't read the letters.
- **More than three @mentions:** warned, because tagging to borrow reach reads as bait.
- **6 to 10 slides in a document:** the report's suggestion for a framework deck, not a finding.
